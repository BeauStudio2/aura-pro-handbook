AI reasoning and model construction

How does AP turn imperfect records into a model the team can inspect, correct and use?

Follow one dispatcher decision from a mistaken queue assignment to corrected evidence, a timed comparison, and later checks. This is the worked AI case for the system architecture; the Learning chapter follows what the team carries forward.

From records to a dependable model

AP’s AI earns a dependable model by making its interpretations inspectable and correctable. It extracts claims from records, connects them to the right objects, compares explanations, and proposes specific changes. People supply context, challenge the interpretation, and direct the work. Calculations and simulations test the consequences of the proposed model; they cannot establish that its starting assumptions are true.

This section follows the dispatcher example in Learning. The records and identifiers below are illustrative additions to that case, not client data or a report of implemented AI performance. The question is time-sensitive: should the dispatcher release the next truck now, or hold it for five minutes?

One queue, several possible explanations

At the decision time, a camera shows six trucks total: L1 is at the pump beginning discharge, and five trucks are waiting in the adjoining staging area. A dispatch export groups all six under the site, without a clear pour assignment. A message says “release the next one.” AP initially treats the six loads as supply for the active pour and predicts unnecessary waiting. The dispatcher corrects it: two trucks serve a different pour.

This initial assignment is a model-construction failure, not an acceptable default. The reusable rule is: a site-level count cannot become pour-specific supply without supported load assignments. Until those links exist, AP can compare possible assignments, but it labels the supply and resulting advice conditional. The human correction exposes the missing check; the revised procedure prevents the same unsupported inference from silently becoming a fact again.

AP retains alternatives: all six really serve this pour; two serve another pour; or an old export or duplicate ticket has inflated the count. It starts with the dispatcher’s explanation and checks current ticket assignments because that check distinguishes these possibilities quickly. A count alone cannot do so. An unreadable identifier stays unresolved; AP does not fill it in from what would make the schedule work.

Resolve identity and propose a model change

A truck can carry several loads in a day. AP identifies the delivery by its ticket and order, using the truck and time as supporting context. It links the camera observation to that delivery only when the evidence supports the match. A forwarded ticket is another copy of the same source, not a second delivery or independent confirmation.

In this example, current tickets connect loads L1–L4 to pour A and L5–L6 to pour B. The dispatcher identifies the two disputed loads and explains that the export groups by site. This supports a narrow correction: the queue count was real, but its assignment to one pour was wrong. It does not establish a new pumping rate or prove that every future export has the same problem.

The physical connection comes from receiving records: at arrival, the receiver records each truck identifier against its current delivery ticket and arrival time. The dispatcher identifies those trucks in the staging area; the time-stamped view and receiving records support the match to L1–L6. For the minute-zero state, the pump lead confirms that L1 was just beginning discharge and L2–L4 were still full, with their delivery checks complete and no intervening departure or unloading recorded before that instant. Ticket quantities support the delivered amounts; remaining quantity is established from discharge records or an explicit estimate. A ticket alone proves neither presence nor remaining supply. An unmatched truck, partial load or uncertain discharge status stays unresolved in the calculation.

The dispatcher resolves “the next one” as load L7 on truck T7, departing the supplier for pour A, using its delivery ticket and order. L7 is separate from the six loads already on site. Before release, the dispatcher confirms the loaded truck is ready, the driver has the destination, and the departure can proceed. The release record names L7, T7, the destination, issuer and time; departure acknowledgment records when the truck actually leaves. If readiness or departure remains unconfirmed, AP cannot assume the journey has begun.

AP proposes a structured change against the current model revision:

  • Replace the inferred assignment. Assign L5 and L6 to pour B using the identified tickets and dispatcher correction. Retain the earlier inference and the reason it was superseded.
  • Recalculate affected supply. Count four loads for A. Recheck B’s queue and any shared dispatch or staging capacity; do not create two new loads or count these loads in both pours.
  • Keep assumptions visible. Remaining quantities, pumping rate, changeovers and incoming travel time still have their own evidence and uncertainty. Correct identity does not validate those inputs.
  • Preserve decision boundaries. Updating the forecast does not issue a truck release, change an approved sequence, or transfer someone’s authority.

Before accepting the change, AP checks the source revisions, identifiers, units, assignment conflicts and the model revision it was based on. If another person has reassigned a load meanwhile, it reconciles that conflict rather than overwriting it. The dispatch lead reviews this first disputed assignment. Later, agreed checks can accept matching current tickets without repeated human confirmation; missing or contradictory evidence reopens the question. Evidence access and permission to use it remain separate from permission to act.

A conflicting reassignment stays open until its scope and effective time are established. AP preserves the competing claims and shows how each affects the choice. The dispatcher can act with uncertainty recorded; that action does not settle the disputed fact. The later L9 example shows how location, assignment and authorization are resolved separately.

The original delivery timeline

The illustrative Learning case follows this sequence. AP retains both when events happened and when their records became available:

  1. Minute 0: L1 begins discharge. The dispatcher releases L7 for pour A and its departure is acknowledged at minute 0, while AP investigates the staged-load assignments.
  2. After departure, before arrival: AP confirms L1–L4 serve A and updates the forecast for the already-traveling L7. The case does not specify the correction’s exact completion time; an actual decision record does.
  3. Minute 37: receiving records confirm L7 arrives at A.
  4. Minute 40: the preceding supply finishes and L7 begins unloading, after three minutes of waiting.

The corrected comparison explains this decision afterward; it did not cause the dispatcher’s earlier release. The five-minute investigation below is a separate simulated branch in which L7 has not yet departed.

Calculate, explain, and let the human decide

AP selects a reviewed delivery-and-consumption model: identified loads supply a pour, pumping consumes their remaining quantity, and incoming travel determines the next arrival. The pump lead checks whether this model fits the method and current conditions, including changeovers and unloading access. AI proposes the mapping from records into that structure; it does not invent the governing relationships afresh from a camera count. Unequal loads, interruptions or additional acceptance delays require corresponding inputs or a more detailed model.

The following table compares the original mistaken model with the corrected starting state at minute zero. It is a retrospective calculation, not a replay of what AP knew at release.

For this simplified comparison, each of the four assigned loads represents ten minutes of pumping supply, including changeovers, measured from the same starting instant. That gives forty minutes of supply. L7 on T7 is ready to depart at the comparison time, and its journey is assumed to take thirty to forty-five minutes. These are illustrative inputs, not fitted probabilities.

Model or actionCalculation from the decision timeConditional consequence
Initial mistaken model: six loads for A6 × 10 = 60 minutes of supply; immediate arrival at minute 30–45.Apparent waiting of 15–30 minutes. This forecast used an incorrect assignment.
Corrected model: release immediately4 × 10 = 40 minutes of supply; arrival at minute 30–45.Up to 10 minutes of truck waiting or 5 minutes of pump idle time.
Corrected model: hold five minutesArrival at 5 + (30–45) = minute 35–50.Up to 5 minutes of truck waiting or 10 minutes of pump idle time.

The calculation uses waiting = max(0, supply end − arrival) and pump idle time = max(0, arrival − supply end). It assumes the truck can unload upon arrival once preceding supply finishes, with no additional access, acceptance or setup delay. If those assumptions are disputed, AP adds the missing constraints or keeps the recommendation conditional. A range alone supplies neither an expected cost nor the probability of a delay.

The corrected comparison favors immediate release of L7 under the dispatcher’s stated priority of maintaining supply: a five-minute hold reduces potential truck waiting but increases potential pump idle time. AP links the tickets, correction, assumptions and calculation so the dispatcher can challenge an input or priority and request another comparison.

The calculation updates the pour’s supply forecast and any dependent crew or equipment use. A whole-project completion date changes only if those effects reach a controlling dependency.

Advance the decision clock

In the separate delayed-decision branch, L7 has not been released, and resolving the assignments takes five minutes. The pump lead’s current report is consistent with uninterrupted pumping at the assumed rate, so supply still ends at minute 40, leaving 35 minutes from now. AP advances consumption from the last supported observation and labels any intervening estimate; it does not reset four full loads at minute five. If pumping has stopped or the report is stale, it revises the state or shows conditional supply ranges.

Choice at minute 5Arrival on the original clockConsequence with supply ending at minute 40
Release now5 + (30–45) = minute 35–50Up to 5 minutes of truck waiting or 10 minutes of pump idle time.
Hold another five minutes10 + (30–45) = minute 40–55No truck waiting in this range; pump idle time of 0–15 minutes.

AP tells the dispatcher: “Five minutes have elapsed. We estimate 35 minutes of supply remaining. Releasing L7 now could leave up to ten minutes without supply; another five-minute hold increases that to fifteen. Maintaining supply still favors release now.” This assumes T7 can depart now and the travel range remains applicable. AP checks departure status and refreshes relevant travel information before issuing the comparison; a truck already en route needs an arrival forecast, not another release instruction. The dispatcher owns the action.

How the AI work is carried out

AP runs this as a bounded inquiry, with a question, responsible person, decision deadline, permitted evidence, competing explanations and next useful check. It saves the current findings, tool results and unresolved work so a delayed import or interrupted session can resume without starting the investigation again. A source correction identifies which conclusions and calculations need another look.

The work is divided by responsibility: extraction produces evidence-linked fields; identity resolution proposes record matches; inquiry selects a discriminating check; planning generates alternatives; calculation tools return results from explicit inputs; and the explanation connects those results to the human decision. These are logical jobs and may use the same model or different tools. Simple parsing, arithmetic and consistency rules do not need a language model. Model outputs must satisfy the required structure and reference valid records before entering the shared model.

AP retrieves only evidence permitted for the question and keeps enough context to preserve its meaning. Text inside an email or document remains source content: an instruction embedded there cannot change the AI’s permissions or execute a command. A claimed approval is checked against its issuer, scope and current authority. A failed extraction or unavailable tool produces an unresolved result and a usable manual path.

The inquiry stops when it has enough evidence for the stated decision, or when more investigation would arrive too late or cost more than it could change. The assigned person sees the unresolved trade and can choose, wait or request another check. AP keeps explanations tied to evidence and calculations rather than presenting fluent prose as proof. Routine checks run under agreed rules; people’s attention goes to consequential uncertainty and choices.

The car-wash simulation, air-handler calculation and whole-development model are tools this reasoning uses. Each run retains its input snapshot, method and version. A valid run can still answer the wrong question if the AI chose the wrong loads, omitted a dependency, or supplied an unsupported assumption.

A later contradiction reopens the question

On a later delivery, after the team has stopped requiring duplicate assignment confirmation, the ticket and dispatch export both assign load L9 to pour A. A new receiving record places that same delivery at pour B, and the dispatcher reports a recent reassignment. AP notices the conflict because it continues comparing received loads and discharge records with forecast supply. Agreement between the ticket and export is not independent confirmation: both inherited the same original assignment.

AP marks the affected supply forecasts for A and B as disputed and withdraws the recommendation that relied on L9 supplying A. It tells the dispatcher and pump lead which decision needs another look, preserving the old forecast and the time of the new report. Once the receiver establishes L9’s identity and arrival at B, AP records that physical location even if the reassignment’s authorization is missing. It retains the recorded assignment to A separately. L9 is no longer counted as physically available at A, and receipt at B does not establish acceptance for use or discharge there. A proposed return to A must include the movement, time and readiness checks it would require. No issued dispatch instruction is silently canceled; the dispatcher decides the response.

Within the agreed exception procedure, AP asks for the dated dispatch change to resolve the authorization question. In this illustrative continuation, that record confirms the authorized reassignment to B, and AP updates the assignment from its effective time while retaining when it learned of the change. If no authorization exists, the observed arrival at B remains true, the assignment discrepancy remains open, and the dispatcher decides what to do next. Location, authorized destination, acceptance and remaining quantity stay separate inputs to the recalculated forecasts for both pours; L9 cannot supply both at once.

The dispatch lead reviews why the old records agreed. Confirmation remains required for pending or conflicting reassignments; unaffected deliveries retain their routine checks. The next applicable reassignment tests whether AP sees the change and receiving acknowledgment before relying on the revised supply forecast. The lead reviews that evidence before relaxing the check.

If confirmation never arrives: each required acknowledgment has an expected time and a responsible person under the agreed workflow. When it is overdue, AP marks arrival, assignment or acceptance as unconfirmed for the relevant scope; two matching old documents do not keep that state current. It checks for a delayed import or an available receiving record first. If the gap could change the next release or the supply forecast, AP asks the receiver to confirm the physical arrival and alerts the dispatcher to the unresolved decision. If no decision is yet affected, it keeps a timed follow-up instead of repeatedly interrupting the team. It preserves the last supported observation and shows conditional forecasts; silence proves neither arrival nor absence. A late confirmation updates the event at its actual time and triggers recalculation without pretending AP knew it earlier.

Evaluating the reasoning

In the original L7 timeline, receiving and discharge records support the minute-37 arrival and minute-40 unloading start. Those results are consistent with the corrected forecast. They do not prove that immediate release is universally best, calibrate a travel-time range from one result, or establish what would have happened after a five-minute hold.

CheckRequired evidence of success
Extraction and identityCorrect fields, units and current revisions; exact supporting source locations; duplicates recognized; ambiguous matches left open. Include cases where the correct assignment cannot yet be known.
Explanation and model changeDetect the site-versus-pour mistake, seek distinguishing evidence, correct only supported claims, and preserve conflicting accounts. A citation must support the particular inference, not merely mention the truck.
Calculation and propagationReproduce the stated comparison, conserve loads across both pours, identify affected forecasts and avoid invented dependencies. A proposed branch changes no live commitment.
Authority and recoveryHandle a concurrent reassignment, a late report, a restricted source and an embedded instruction without bypassing permissions or losing the human’s decision. Missing evidence or a tool failure remains visible.
Decision time and later contradictionsAdvance supply and travel state during investigation; distinguish a retrospective corrected-state calculation from advice available at release. Introduce a receiving conflict or overdue confirmation, retain observed location separately from authorization, mark affected recommendations unusable, preserve issued actions, and identify the next check and its owner.
Usefulness to the teamMeasure correction effort, unnecessary questions, advice delivered before the decision deadline, and actual waiting and idle time. Compare with the team’s ordinary process under comparable conditions.

These are evaluation requirements, not reported results. Test each stage and the complete inquiry on independently reviewed cases withheld from tuning, including cases where the proper answer is “unknown.” Historical replays expose only records available at the decision time. Report consequential false matches and missed conditions separately from average accuracy; a plausible final answer can conceal compensating mistakes. Preserve model, extraction, prompt, retrieval and calculation versions so a regression can be located and reversed.

Learning changes the appropriate part of the system. The assignment correction updates this project’s facts. Repeated timing evidence may update a numerical forecast. The dispatch lead’s accepted assignment-check procedure changes inquiry and observation rules. A communication preference updates the team relationship. Improving retrieval or extraction is evaluated separately; none of these changes automatically requires retraining a language model or expands its authority.

The next applicable delivery tests whether AP checks the right assignments without a redundant question. Later deliveries test the timing model. A separately agreed smaller-queue trial tests a new operating practice. The Learning dashboard retains what the human and AP each learned, what remains uncertain, and who will review the next result.

What this case establishes

A model becomes useful through supported identities, explicit assumptions, timely calculations and human correction. Later evidence can change it again.
