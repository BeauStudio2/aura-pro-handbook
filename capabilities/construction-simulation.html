Simulating a whole building project

This proposal shows how Aura Pro would use project histories and NVIDIA tools to simulate construction from the site as it stands today to the building the owner wants to open. Compare how to get there, find out which assumptions matter, and update the plan as the work unfolds.

What to build

Aura Pro should own the model of how the project gets built. Give it one clock for the development, one account of the resources each plan uses, and an explicit definition of what the owner will accept. Use NVIDIA to answer questions inside that model. Can the rigged unit turn through this opening? Which truck dispatches can reach a free unloading bay? Would a different weather pattern remove the only workable lift day? Each answer must be able to change the installation sequence, cost and opening forecast.

The scheduler should be able to put two buildable plans in front of the team, point to the assumption that separates them, and ask the person who can check it. The superintendent should be able to suggest a third plan. The owner should see the trade between opening certainty, cash required and obligations carried into occupancy. A plan can win on one measure and lose on another. The team defines the priorities and the limits a plan must meet.

There is a construction precedent. Bouygues combines building models, equipment models and terrain in custom Omniverse applications. In a separate stadium field test, Obayashi linked its building model to crane positions and worker activity. The first shows how to assemble a common spatial model. The second shows how to update it from site observations. Predicting a completion date from those observations requires the labor, commercial and approval model described below. What can change the outcome

Work backward from conditions of use. For a phased opening, name the floors, access, utilities, systems, tests, approvals and owner acceptance required to occupy them while construction continues elsewhere. Then build forward from the dated site conditions. Track design, procurement and fieldwork concurrently wherever the chosen contracts and methods allow. The following questions belong in the same project model.

Where uncertainty entersWhat to represent and what it can change
Development, finance and consentLand access, financing draws, cash on hand, tenant commitments, scope changes, public objections, agency decisions and appeals. A delayed consent can allow off-site design to continue while excavation and financing dates move. Model a specific hearing or decision through which political conditions could affect work.
Design and procurementDesign maturity by package, open questions, releases, submittals, vendor capacity, fabrication slots, substitutions, freight, damage and replacement lead times. An early order can secure a slot and expose the owner to redesign. A signed order, a shipped unit and a usable unit on its floor are distinct states.

A poor soil finding can change the method across the footprint. A floor poured yesterday can still be unavailable for loading.
People and organizationsTrade and skill mix, qualified supervision, shift and union rules, dispatch and absence, repeated-floor learning, fatigue, communication, shared crews, disputes and remobilization. A second shift may add hours while a shared hoist or a missing qualified operator holds production flat.
Equipment, supply and movementSupplier, truck, street permit, gate, unloading bay, staging area, lift, travel path and receiving crew. Include breakdowns, repair parts, setup and return trips. A blocked laydown area can hold trucks in the street and consume the next trade’s delivery window.
Enclosure, systems and interiorsWeather exposure, floor handovers, available power and water, access panels, shaft occupancy, trade stacking, inspections before concealment and damaged or rejected work. An early wall closure may protect finishes and block a later equipment route.

Commissioning, occupancy and handoverPermanent utility connections, integrated tests, failed tests, corrective work, specialist bookings, inspections, partial occupancy conditions, documentation, training and asset acceptance. A one-day slip across a booked test window can move opening by weeks. Keep unresolved obligations attached to the asset after opening.

How to keep finding what is missing. For each package ask what has to arrive, fit, be understood, be paid for, be available and be accepted. Ask the trades what stopped the last comparable job. Record each plausible cause with its affected work, source, earliest observable signal, owner and possible response. Use a fixed constraint for a known limit, a range or distribution for measured variation, and named scenarios for plausible events with weak frequency data. Keep unresolved questions visible. An unexplained delay or failed test opens a new inquiry and can add a dependency the model has never represented.

How previous projects shape the next one

Previous projects have two jobs. They tell us what ought to be simulated and give us starting estimates for conditions we cannot yet observe. The current project supplies its own geometry, issued design, contracts, available money, commitments, crews, installed quantities and bookings as of a stated date.

  1. Reconstruct the history as people experienced it. Collect successive schedules and calendars, drawing revisions and decisions from Procore, daily hours, quantities and site observations from Raken, and commitments and actual costs from Sage. Join these to purchase, delivery, inspection, test and occupancy records by project, package, zone, trade, asset and date. Keep the original record, its revision, when the event happened and when the team learned of it. Track unresolved or ambiguous matches.
  2. Discover mechanisms. Look for repeated waits, broken sequences, rework and differences between planned and actual production. If several lifts missed their slots, examine the delivery tickets, hoist logs and daily narratives with the people who ran the work. Truck lateness, a blocked gate and an unavailable receiving crew call for different responses. Text extraction can propose causes and cite records. A planner or trade lead checks the causal link before it drives scenarios.
  3. Estimate comparable rates and uncertainty. Group completed work by method, quantity, location, building type, crew mix, season and access conditions. Estimate productive hours per installed unit separately from waiting, setup and rework. An open permit contributes the fact that it has already waited this long. A missing daily report contributes a gap. Include interrupted and unsuccessful jobs. Where close matches are sparse, draw on broader comparable work, widen the range and record which expert supplied any adjustment.
  4. Initialize this job and give conflicting evidence to an owner. Use a site walk, current register and date-stamped records to establish what is complete, in transit, held, booked or still undecided. Where records disagree, run both plausible states until someone checks. A comparable tower can supply a bracket-tolerance range. Only a measurement, or other accepted evidence on this tower, establishes the installed opening. For a project already under way, preserve finished work and sunk costs and estimate the remaining quantity.

Public sources fill particular gaps. Cambridge publishes approximately 300 construction schedules that can help test dependency and duration models. NYC filings and occupancy records supply public administrative endpoints. Historical station weather supplies sequences of shared exposure. Use the client’s linked records to establish which crews, deliveries and decisions produced a particular result. Keep project histories within their permitted uses, and carry sample size, source population and uncertainty into each reused estimate. How a whole-project run works

  1. Freeze the starting state. Give the run a dated snapshot, scope, success criteria, calendars and versioned assumptions. Keep separate the conditions in the simulated world, the information each participant has received and the actions each can authorize. In a historical replay, a Friday report cannot inform a Tuesday decision.
  2. Generate alternatives with the team. The superintendent proposes methods, the trades specify prerequisites, and the scheduler uses a constraint solver to explore order, crew assignments, batches, shifts and reservations. Fix irreversible work and commitments. Keep several feasible choices and explicit responses such as “if this route fails before panel close-in, hold a second bay.” Compare expected cost, opening reliability, peak cash and disruption under the owner’s priorities. Reject any option that violates a hard condition.
  3. Run each choice through the same possible futures. A discrete-event engine advances the clock to the next delivery, shift, completed quantity, hold, release or decision. A task starts when its prerequisites, qualified people, equipment, work area, materials and permissions coincide. Waiting consumes elapsed time and may incur standby cost. Rejection creates corrective work and a new inspection. Sample shared shocks and local variation together. Give competing plans the same weather and supplier scenarios so the comparison measures the sequence.
  4. Open a detailed model where the answer matters. A floor can begin as one production cycle. If hoist contention controls occupancy, replace that cycle with trips, loads, stops, workers and time windows. The detailed model borrows the same hoist, inventory and crew from the project and returns elapsed time, consumption and observations. It replaces the relevant allowance in the parent calculation. Completed history stays fixed. A physical question can use seconds of motion inside a project that spans years.
  5. Find the next useful decision. Report a range of opening dates and costs, the chance of meeting the target where probabilities are calibrated, and which dependencies control the result across runs. Vary suspect inputs to see which can reverse the preferred sequence. Compare the value of checking now with the cost of checking and the time left to act. If every plausible width admits the load, move on. If the width changes a three-week decision, send the foreman a precise question and a deadline.

Distinguish a better sequence from a lucky run. Optimize on one set of possible futures, then compare the shortlisted plans on a fresh set. Schedule changes inside a run can use only the information that has arrived by then. Record the frequency with which each bottleneck controls opening. An available test slot might control most runs while a late utility connection controls the expensive tail. Ask how the recommendation changes if both happen together. This lets the team design contingencies for several paths through the same development.

For each option the scheduler gets the input revision, dispatch and fallback rules, failed constraints, affected floors, resource peaks, cost exposure and the conditions under which the recommendation changes. The owner can choose a more expensive sequence that preserves a tenant commitment. If two plans are indistinguishable within the uncertainty, keep both available until a decision is due. How labor and physical logistics interact

Model a crew by the work it can perform under stated conditions. A concrete pour needs a foreman, people able to place and finish, an operator, a running pump, truck access and an area released for pouring. Represent travel, setup, breaks, productive time, cleanup and handoff. On repetitive floors the crew can learn. Changing the crew mix, overtime or sharing a workface may change the rate again. Estimate these effects against recorded quantities and hours, and let the foreman challenge the assumptions. Split out an individual role when that role can hold the operation, as with the only qualified operator on a shift.

Consider the handbook’s Galleria estimate. An additional 50 cubic yards at the assumed North Lex rate of 0.8 labor-hours per cubic yard gives 40 additional labor-hours. At that rate, five workers would need eight hours together. Check how much ordinary waiting the historical rate already includes before modeling extra delays. Elapsed time also depends on the permitted pour window, truck headway, discharge rate, pump capacity and finishing space. Sample late deliveries and unloading queues, reserve the pump and crew on the common calendar, and apply the project’s actual pour-break rules if the window closes. Only then can the model say whether stripping forms, the next floor cycle or opening moves. North Lex to Galleria estimate

For example, assume one truck can unload at a time, each truck carries 10 cubic yards, trucks arrive every 15 minutes and unloading takes 20 minutes. Five loads deliver the extra 50 cubic yards. The unloading bay can handle 30 cubic yards per hour in steady operation, while dispatch sends 40. The fifth truck arrives 60 minutes after the first and waits 20 minutes to start unloading. Sending more trucks increases waiting. Placement labor and unloading time overlap, so the engine schedules them on the same timeline. These numbers illustrate the queue. Actual tickets, batch sizes, pour method and unloading times would set the inputs for Galleria.

People also change the plan. Give the superintendent rules for redeploying an idle crew, the supplier rules for rescheduling a rejected delivery, and the reviewer a queue and response time. A foreman can spot a conflict, wait for an answer, proceed under an agreed instruction or propose another method. The team can play these roles in a run and compare its decisions with the modeled response. Use crew-level evidence for productivity. An avatar walking across a floor tells us little about how quickly a trade can install and pass inspection.

Where NVIDIA fits

Use Omniverse where a decision depends on the changing physical site. Evaluate the other tools against a specific project question and a simpler alternative. All of them can operate behind Aura Pro. Planners and crews can continue using the project’s existing tools.

Building and equipment geometry · Omniverse, OpenUSD and RTX

Recommended spatial integration. Read the building information model and schedule links through IFC where available. Use NVIDIA’s OpenUSD Exchange SDK to build a scene from the relevant design elements, terrain, supplier models and temporary works. Keep design revisions, measured conditions and proposed sequences in separate layers. A view for the lift day should include the installed bracket, scaffolding, protection, rigging and the panels actually scheduled to be in place. Use Omniverse Kit and RTX rendering when the team needs to inspect the route together. The same identifiers should support a headless dimension check or an ordinary IFC viewer. OpenUSD Exchange SDK · Omniverse libraries · IfcOpenShell scheduling tools

Clearance and motion · PhysX and Warp

Start with arithmetic where it decides the question. For a load that must rotate past an obstruction, test the volume it occupies throughout its path. A PhysX collision model can evaluate moving rigid bodies. Warp is an option for custom, repeated geometry queries across possible poses and offsets. Check how the collision mesh represents narrow gaps and whether the result changes with time step or tolerance. Export minimum clearance, location of closest approach, assumed load orientation and bounds on measurement error. For rigging forces, ground bearing, stability or member capacity, use the equipment’s load chart and the lift engineer’s applicable calculations or specialist solver. A collision-free animation only answers a geometric question. Omni Physics collision behavior · Warp documentation

Deliveries and site traffic · cuOpt

Use cuOpt to generate delivery routes from vehicle capacities, time windows, pickup and delivery pairs, service times and travel times by vehicle type. The project engine supplies current gate availability and reserves the receiving resources. Test the returned dispatch against unloading queues, full staging areas and shared hoist capacity. When conditions change, freeze trips already under way and solve the remaining work again. Compare with OR-Tools and the dispatcher’s plan on the same site. Adopt cuOpt where it returns more useful feasible choices in the time available to dispatch. cuOpt routing features · cuOpt guidance on changing constraints

Weather and engineering response · Earth-2 and PhysicsNeMo

For years of construction, sample seasonally appropriate historical weather sequences. Near execution, compare available local forecasts with Earth2Studio ensembles, then update with site observations. Apply a shared weather realization to freight, lifts, exposed interiors and curing. Translating regional wind to gusts at the hook height needs site exposure, elevation and a checked local relationship. The lift team supplies equipment-specific limits and makes the day-of-work decision. Test whether Earth-2 improves decisions over the local forecast at the relevant lead time. Earth2Studio · NVIDIA’s observation and downscaling example

PhysicsNeMo is worth testing when a repeated engineering calculation is too slow to include across many sequences. For example, train a fast approximation of an established airflow solver across enclosure states and weather conditions, then validate it against withheld simulations and measured conditions. Return predicted effects with error bounds and the range of inputs on which the model was tested. Use the original solver or engineer’s assessment outside that range. PhysicsNeMo · NVIDIA’s engineering simulation example

Field observations and autonomous equipment · DeepStream, Isaac Sim and Cosmos

If gate queues drive the schedule and tickets cannot resolve them, DeepStream can process camera feeds for truck arrivals, departures and bay occupancy. Compare the resulting counts with time-stamped dispatch logs and sampled human counts through rain, darkness and occlusion. A truck visible at the gate establishes arrival. Delivery acceptance still needs the receiver’s record. Use aggregated counts when that answers the question. Site logs and manual samples are also valid inputs. NVIDIA DeepStream

Use Isaac Sim for a robotic layout cart, autonomous hauler or a sensor whose behavior affects the schedule. Its Replicator tooling and Cosmos can generate varied scenes for testing perception. The Mega blueprint illustrates multiple robots interacting in a facility. Return measured cycle-time ranges, blockage behavior or detector performance to the project engine. A synthetic video is useful for testing recognition under poor lighting. Estimating the actual frequency of that condition or its effect on labor requires jobsite evidence. If the building uses conventional equipment, crew and equipment records can supply the cycle model directly. Isaac Sim · Mega blueprint · Cosmos synthetic data workflows

Historical data and specialist buildings · RAPIDS and DSX

Benchmark RAPIDS cuDF for large joins and aggregations across prior jobs, such as matching delivery events to weather and shifts. Compare end-to-end time and cost with the existing database or a CPU dataframe implementation, including data transfer. Run independent project simulations on ordinary CPU workers wherever that is faster or less expensive. For a data-center development, the DSX blueprint offers a reference for linking power and thermal simulations to facility components. Borrow that integration pattern for specialist commissioning loads when the building warrants it. cuDF documentation · Omniverse DSX blueprint

Connecting the tools

One project record, interchangeable calculations. Store project facts, relationships, dated events, assumptions and decision authority in the Aura Pro data model. Store large drawings, scenes and simulation outputs as versioned files linked to those records. Map a source element, schedule activity, purchase line, work area and crew assignment through stable IDs. Preserve units, coordinate origin, vertical datum, time zone, calendars, revision and survey tolerance. Check known site dimensions after import. An attractive model built in the wrong units can reverse a decision.

One tower, two opening dates

Start with occupancy. In the handbook’s illustrative tower, the lower floors need working ventilation, completed tests and the other occupancy requirements by October 1. Aura Pro compares lifting the assembled air handler before enclosure with giving the façade crew the crane first. Enclosing first protects winter interior work and keeps ventilation tests booked to finish September 30. That sequence depends on the assembled unit passing through one remaining bay. The scheduler asks for the installed clearance while the foreman can still hold back the adjoining panel.

The field changes the model. A bracket leaves 99½ inches clear, with ¼ inch measurement uncertainty. Even the upper bound of 99¾ inches is below the supplier’s 100-inch minimum. That rules out this route before spending time on a motion simulation. The foreman records the finding in Raken. The lift lead repeats the check and verifies the wider route. Where the turn or rigging envelope needs analysis, the spatial service can test the measured route against the temporary works and return its tightest point for the lift lead to check.

Run the development again. Two bays let the assembled unit use the booked crane date and keep the September tests. Delivering the unit in sections solves the access problem too, but assembly finishes too late for that booking. The next tests finish October 21. With other occupancy work on track, opening follows on October 22. The 21-day difference comes from the test calendar. It would change if a different commissioning slot or another controlling dependency emerged.

Return the choice to the people who can act. The design reviewer accepts the wider opening and protection. Protection costs $4,000 against $3,000 in remaining delegated authority, so the project executive signs and the subcontractor countersigns. The project manager records the change in Procore. After schedule approval and publication, the superintendent releases the crew and accounting records the commitment in Sage. The scheduler uses the actual lift, close-in and test dates to update occupancy. The owner’s representative checks the opening. The retained record tells a future facilities manager which route worked, under which conditions, and what to measure before ordering a replacement. How to tell whether the predictions help

Replay an unseen development from several decision dates. Train on other projects, freeze what was knowable on each date, and forecast the remaining work. Compare the approved schedule, the scheduler’s contemporary forecast, the CPU model, and the same model with the proposed NVIDIA calculation. Score the actual path against observed dates, costs and resource use. An unchosen sequence has no observed opening date. Evaluate its constraints with the trades, vary the assumptions and label its result as a modeled alternative.

  • Does the model obey the site? Try a double-booked crane, an unapproved pour, an empty staging area and a route just above and below its measured clearance. Check material conservation, crew hours, infeasible routes, test bookings and cost when an activity is expanded into tasks. A missing physical check must appear as unresolved.
  • Do the forecasts match actual experience? Measure date and cost error by horizon and phase, queue and standby time by resource, and prediction ranges against actual outcomes. Across comparable held-out forecasts, about 80 percent of outcomes should fall on or before a calibrated 80th-percentile date. Report how wide the ranges are and how many independent projects support the comparison. Report extreme scenarios separately when their probabilities are unknown.
  • Does a specialist tool earn its place? Compare route predictions with surveyed clearances and observed trips, dispatch plans with dispatcher results, and weather predictions with site measurements. Track false feasibility, missed constraints, recommendation changes, runtime, and cost per usable answer. Run enough independent futures to see whether the ranking is stable. Record actual solver outputs as well as seeds when the solver can vary between runs.
  • Does the team get a better decision in time? In live advisory use, preserve the original forecast, when the alert arrived, the alternatives still available, who acted, and the eventual outcome. Trace a miss back to its cause. Check the starting state, dependencies, production rates, timing of reports and how the team responded. Change that part of the model, then rerun earlier and held-out cases. Keep an unused warning or an incorrect recommendation in the record.

Separate learning about this site from learning across jobs. A confirmed obstruction updates this project immediately. One slow pour prompts a review of dispatch, access and crew hours. Repeated, comparable observations can change the production-rate estimate for future floors. Before carrying the change into other developments, check it against jobs with a different mix of crews and methods. Overtime often appears on jobs already behind schedule. That association alone cannot predict what adding a shift would accomplish here. Preserve the earlier model and its forecasts so the team can see whether the revision improved later decisions.

Evidence needed from the client. Request a linked, permissioned set of dated schedule revisions, daily reports with installed quantities and crew hours, drawing and model revisions, supplier and delivery events, payment and commitment records, test bookings and actual occupancy. Ask the scheduler, superintendent, trade leads, commissioning lead and project accountant to reconstruct the same decision independently. Gaps in that reconstruction identify the measurements worth collecting on the next job. This proposal evaluates documented tool capabilities. Forecast accuracy, integration cost and GPU performance on the client’s projects remain to be measured.
