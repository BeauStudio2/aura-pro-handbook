Work packages and whole-development simulation

Case: the week-numbered development

Compare equipment strategies, funding and occupancy from the current state. This is separate from the introductory October tower. The logistics and engineering chapters continue its sectional-equipment branch.

Aura Pro models the development as it stands today, the outcome it must achieve, and the possible paths between them. Work packages describe the actions along those paths. Shared models describe how the building, resources, finances, environment, and people respond.

The purpose is to develop an executable strategy, test its assumptions, and revise it as evidence arrives. A change in procurement can alter design, funding, construction methods, and occupancy. AP follows those connections and helps the team decide what to do.

This worked example illustrates AP’s decision process. Its dates, costs, supplier arrangements, and findings are hypothetical, chosen to make the reasoning inspectable; they are not results from a completed simulation run. References to observations and commitments describe their status within the example.

Establish the outcome and current state

Consider a 20-story office development approaching structural completion. Façade and interior work are progressing. Major equipment is ordered. The owner’s target is occupancy in week 16, measured from today.

Occupancy requires completed spaces, functioning systems, utilities, required permissions, and an operating team ready to take responsibility. AP represents these conditions separately so it can explain what controls completion.

Its starting state includes:

  • Physical work, geometry, access, temporary works, and defects.
  • Released designs, unresolved choices, and required performance.
  • People, equipment, materials, suppliers, and calendars.
  • Contracts, commitments, payment terms, and available funding.
  • Permissions, inspections, testing, and acceptance requirements.
  • The evidence supporting each assertion and the uncertainty that remains.

The owner establishes how to judge alternatives. In this example, earlier occupancy has value, but the date is negotiable. The team will consider additional expenditure against delay costs while preserving required building performance.

Separate reality, understanding, and simulated futures

AP maintains three connected records:

Record What it contains
Actual project record Accepted facts, completed work, issued instructions, reservations, and commitments.
Current understanding Reports, assumptions, disputed information, and uncertain forecasts.
Simulated branches The consequences of particular methods, decisions, and external conditions.

A supplier quotation does not establish a reservation. A selected strategy does not establish a purchase. A simulated crane allocation does not reserve the crane.

AP distinguishes assumed availability, availability reported by a provider, a conditional hold, and a secured commitment. It records the conditions and expiration of each. A secured commitment still carries execution uncertainty; it does not guarantee performance.

An alternative progresses through qualification, selection, commitment, work release, execution, and acceptance. Each transition has conditions and a responsible person. Some transitions make later changes expensive or impossible.

Define work packages and their connections

A work package describes an intended change, available methods, and the conditions under which each method works.

Element What it establishes
Result and acceptance What must change, who accepts it, and what that acceptance permits.
Starting conditions Required physical conditions, information, permissions, and predecessor results.
Methods Alternative ways to achieve the result and their consequences.
Resources People, equipment, materials, space, utilities, information, and funding.
Behavior Setup, production, interruption, restart, rework, and resource release.
Decisions Who selects, commits, releases, and handles exceptions.
Observation What needs checking and which findings could change the plan.
Effects Changes to physical state, resource availability, cost, cash, and downstream obligations.

Packages nest:

Building ready for occupancy
└── Ventilation system accepted
    ├── Equipment and interfaces approved
    ├── Equipment procured
    ├── Supports and installation access accepted
    ├── Equipment installed and connected
    ├── Controls integrated
    ├── System tested and balanced
    └── Performance accepted and operating team prepared

The hierarchy describes scope. Connections across it describe execution.

Ventilation work shares the crane with façade installation, electricians with other systems, and access routes with deliveries. All packages draw from the same resource records within a simulation branch.

Parent completion requires explicit acceptance conditions, including interfaces between children. Installed components do not automatically constitute a functioning system.

Behavior models also describe changes outside active work: curing, deterioration, weather, supplier production, and the expiration of offers or permissions.

Begin a complete decision cycle

The original air-handling unit was expected in week 6. Its supplier now reports delivery in week 14.

AP records this as a revised supplier forecast, with its supporting evidence. It identifies the affected installation, controls, testing, and occupancy conditions.

The team develops three alternatives:

  1. Retain the original unit.
  2. Buy an available larger assembled unit.
  3. Buy a manufacturer-approved sectional alternative and assemble it inside.

AP helps qualify each alternative through supplier information, design review, installation planning, commercial review, and team experience.

All alternatives are compared from today forward. Costs already incurred are excluded. Recoverable deposits, cancellation charges, avoided original payments, and replacement payments are accounted for explicitly. Costs common to every alternative are excluded from the comparison.

For this example, the owner estimates that each week beyond the week-16 target adds $70,000 in carrying costs and lost net operating income. The revenue portion assumes the relevant leasing arrangements allow income to begin when occupancy becomes possible. AP uses the project’s actual payment and leasing terms when calculating these effects.

Additional strategy costs include net equipment changes, design, installation, temporary works, and strategy-specific financing. The weekly delay allowance excludes those same charges, preventing double-counting.

Why AP initially prefers the assembled substitute

The initial comparison is:

Alternative Illustrative occupancy Additional strategy cost Delay cost beyond week 16 Combined additional cost
Retain original unit Week 22 $0 $420,000 $420,000
Larger assembled substitute Week 16 $180,000 $0 $180,000
Sectional alternative Week 18 $200,000 $140,000 $340,000

The assembled substitute appears preferable: it preserves the target date and has the lowest combined additional cost in this reference scenario.

Its recommendation is conditional. The larger unit must pass through the retained façade opening, its design interfaces must be acceptable, and there must be a workable route to funding, delivery, installation, and testing commitments.

The sectional alternative takes longer because it requires assembly and additional checks inside the building. Its proposed sequence supports testing in weeks 15–16, acceptance in week 17, and occupancy in week 18.

At this point, the original equipment is under contract. Replacement delivery positions are supplier offers, and the testing provider has reported availability for the relevant blocks. Neither replacement equipment nor replacement testing appointments are yet secured.

These dates come from proposed work sequences and reported calendar availability. They remain conditional forecasts.

Identify the uncertainty that could reverse the choice

The assembled unit’s access assessment relies on design geometry that has not been verified against the installed structure.

AP tests the importance of that uncertainty: if the route fails, the installation method and testing date could change enough to reverse the recommendation.

A survey is therefore more valuable than refining minor labor estimates. AP asks the superintendent to arrange it, with the installation lead responsible for evaluating the result.

The survey is scheduled before the equipment order becomes non-cancellable.

This is the refinement rule: investigate an unknown when its plausible outcomes could change feasibility, the preferred action, or a consequential result. Use the least costly credible way to resolve it—measurement, inquiry, calculation, or decision.

New evidence changes the preferred strategy

The survey establishes that the assembled substitute cannot pass through the retained opening. Installed structural bracing also prevents simply opening the adjacent bay.

The finding settles the surveyed geometry. It invalidates the proposed access method. It does not invalidate the equipment’s previously reviewed performance.

The structural and installation teams develop a temporary support and enlarged-opening method. Their revised sequence requires design, fabrication, installation, and inspection before the lift.

Under that method:

  • The equipment lift moves to week 12.
  • Installation and connections finish at the end of week 15.
  • The unit misses the testing block that starts in week 15.
  • The next suitable block reported as available occupies weeks 19–20.
  • Acceptance occurs in week 21, supporting occupancy in week 22.

In this example, testing requires a coordinated specialist team for a complete two-week sequence. Starting partway through the week-15 block would not complete that sequence within the team’s availability. AP also investigates whether another qualified team or testing arrangement could improve the result.

The retained original unit is forecast to be ready by the end of week 17. It also uses the available weeks 19–20 testing block, followed by acceptance in week 21 and occupancy in week 22.

The revised assembled-unit strategy increases its additional cost to $300,000.

AP reruns the comparison:

Alternative Revised occupancy Additional strategy cost Delay cost Combined additional cost
Retain original unit Week 22 $0 $420,000 $420,000
Larger assembled substitute Week 22 $300,000 $420,000 $720,000
Sectional alternative Week 18 $200,000 $140,000 $340,000

The sectional alternative now appears preferable. AP recommends completing the checks and negotiations needed to make it executable. The evidence has changed the proposed equipment method, funding needs, construction sequence, and intended occupancy date.

Resolve design and funding together

The sectional alternative requires revised duct adapters, support details, electrical connections, controls integration, and manufacturer-supervised assembly.

Those are linked work packages with named design reviewers and acceptance conditions.

The supplier requires a $250,000 deposit by the end of week 2 to secure the offered delivery position. This is an earlier cash requirement, credited against the equipment purchase. It is not another $250,000 added to the strategy cost.

The lender has indicated that funds could be released in week 3 after receiving approved change documentation. That timing is not yet a confirmed funding commitment.

AP exposes the dependency:

Equipment commitment requires cash before the expected lender release; the lender release requires approved change documentation.

The team considers negotiated payment terms, an owner-funded bridge, and losing the delivery position.

The proposed arrangement is a $250,000 owner-funded bridge, repaid when lender funds arrive. Its assumed expense is included in the $200,000 strategy cost. AP also evaluates a later lender release because that would extend the owner’s exposure and could increase financing costs.

Before the order is placed:

  • The design reviewers resolve compatibility questions that could invalidate the purchase.
  • The finance lead obtains written lender conditions and confirms the release process.
  • The owner approves the bridge and confirms that funds are available for transfer.
  • The commercial lead confirms the supplier’s offer, payment deadline, and order terms.
  • The commissioning lead establishes whether the required testing block can be secured.
  • The authorized decision-maker accepts any remaining exposure before approving the commitment.

Where equipment and testing cannot be secured simultaneously, AP shows the risk of committing to one without the other. The team can seek conditional orders, temporary holds, or cancellation terms, or explicitly accept the remaining exposure.

Payment and supplier acknowledgment establish the equipment commitment. Detailed installation matters that cannot invalidate the purchase may remain open, with owners and deadlines before work release.

Revise construction while preserving shared resources

The sectional method contains nested packages for delivery, internal transport, assembly, connections, controls, and testing.

Its largest modules fit the verified transport route and available handling equipment. The installation lead accepts the handling method.

Using this route avoids the enlarged façade opening and allows the façade package to retain its planned closure sequence.

The internal transport still competes with other work. In this illustrative plan, module movements displace two days of finish-material deliveries. The affected finishes had five days of available flexibility. Rescheduling consumes two days and leaves three; it does not move their completion beyond the revised occupancy requirement.

AP also checks the assembly crew, manufacturer attendance, receiving area, electricians, and testing appointments together. Their individual availability is insufficient unless the required combinations can occur.

The sectional unit is forecast to be ready for testing by the end of week 13. Week 14 provides one week of recovery time before testing begins in week 15.

Explain the occupancy forecast and its conditions

The proposed ventilation sequence is:

Work or acceptance Timing
Purchase-critical design approval and equipment commitment By end of week 2
Expected lender funding and bridge repayment Week 3
Sectional equipment delivery Week 9
Assembly and connections Weeks 10–13
Recovery time before testing Week 14
Ventilation testing Weeks 15–16
Acceptance and occupancy readiness review Week 17
Occupancy Week 18

The following readiness dates belong to the sectional strategy. AP distinguishes dates that change with that strategy from independent constraints that apply to every alternative.

Requirement Sectional-strategy forecast Relationship to the strategy and remaining conditions
Permanent power and water End of week 12 Independent constraint shared by all alternatives. Provider dates are reported; connection work and successful energization remain outstanding.
Other systems and interfaces needed for ventilation testing End of week 14 Strategy-dependent electrical and controls work is included. The initial assembled-unit strategy assumed these interfaces ready by week 12. Completion and results remain unverified.
Required system tests End of week 16 Independent tests are forecast complete by week 14. Tests involving ventilation follow its selected sequence and finish in week 16 under the sectional strategy. Specialist appointments must be secured.
Occupied areas, access, and required site work End of week 16 The sectional forecast includes its delivery and work-area changes. The initial assembled-unit strategy assumed these areas ready by week 15. AP recalculates affected work for each alternative.
Operating-team training and handover information End of week 17 Strategy-dependent final activities follow successful testing. They were forecast for week 15 under the initial assembled-unit strategy.
Required permissions and final acceptance End of week 17 Depends on completed evidence and review availability. The initial assembled-unit strategy assumed week-15 acceptance. Neither date is treated as an independently guaranteed appointment.

These forecasts make week-18 occupancy feasible in the sectional reference scenario. The initial week-16 forecast for the assembled unit used its own earlier testing, handover, and acceptance sequence; it did not inherit the sectional strategy’s later dates.

Ventilation completion drives several final readiness activities. Other requirements can independently control occupancy if they slip. AP shows both the dependency chain and any conditions that share control of the opening date.

A utility delay, unresolved system defect, unavailable inspector, or incomplete access route could become the controlling condition. AP updates the entire readiness comparison when one changes.

Alternatives are evaluated under the same external scenarios so that one strategy does not win merely because it received more favorable assumptions.

Show when the recommendation should change again

This comparison concerns the development. The portfolio view also checks displaced work and shared funding or resources across other undertakings. The value review distinguishes this strategy’s forecast advantage from the contribution attributable to AP.

The sectional alternative saves four weeks against retaining the original unit at an additional strategy cost of $200,000.

Its economic break-even point is therefore $50,000 per week of avoided delay. At the illustrative $70,000 weekly value, it has an $80,000 advantage.

That advantage is material but not overwhelming. If occupancy slipped one additional week without changing other costs, the advantage would fall to $10,000. Two additional weeks would reverse it. Missing a testing block could create a larger jump, so AP must recalculate the sequence rather than assume every interruption produces an equal occupancy delay.

AP separates three decisions:

Decision point Recommendation or required action
Recommend a strategy now Provisionally prefer the sectional alternative under the stated assumptions. Complete the checks and negotiations needed to make it executable.
Commit money and contractual obligations Confirm purchase-critical compatibility, acceptable terms, available funding, and the arrangements for securing delivery and testing. The authorized person accepts any remaining exposure before committing.
Release physical work Confirm the detailed method, accepted supports and access, actual resource arrangements, and applicable work-release conditions. The superintendent authorizes the work.

AP presents the conditions that could reverse its preference:

  • Reduced value of earlier occupancy.
  • Increased design, assembly, financing, or cancellation costs.
  • Loss of the delivery position.
  • Failure to secure the testing block.
  • Another development constraint eliminating the occupancy advantage.

Before commitment, these findings can trigger a fresh selection. After commitment, AP evaluates the remaining choices using the costs and obligations that now exist. It cannot assume that cancelling the substitute restores the original option unchanged.

Distinguish observed learning from simulated comparison

As work proceeds, AP compares predictions with outcomes.

It can directly learn the surveyed geometry, actual design turnaround, payment timing, delivery performance, assembly effort, resource conflicts, and test results.

These findings have different implications:

  • A measurement corrects this project’s state.
  • An execution result tests a prediction under particular conditions.
  • Repeated, comparable evidence may justify changing a more general model.

If assembly takes longer than expected, AP distinguishes productive work from waiting, access restrictions, missing information, and rework. It updates the current forecast immediately while investigating which explanation should alter future models.

The unchosen alternatives remain simulated. Successful week-18 occupancy would not prove that the original equipment would have produced week-22 occupancy, or that the substitute was superior in every relevant circumstance.

AP preserves:

  • What actually happened.
  • What the team knew when it decided.
  • What each model predicted.
  • Which assumptions were confirmed or contradicted.
  • What remains an unobserved comparison.

The resulting lesson may improve future estimates or change what AP asks the team to verify before commitment.

Use specialist simulation where it changes the decision

NVIDIA tools can contribute bounded calculations inside this cycle.

Omniverse/OpenUSD can support inspection of the installation scene. Warp can support custom geometry calculations where many paths or configurations need evaluation. cuOpt can help with supported delivery-routing and optimization problems. Weather workflows and trained physical models can contribute where their outputs materially affect the alternatives.

For this particular decision, a verified survey and a straightforward dimensional check may settle the first access question. More elaborate simulation becomes useful if the remaining route involves complex movement, tolerances, or temporary configurations.

Every specialist result returns its inputs, assumptions, model version, findings, and unresolved uncertainty to AP. AP retains the development model, shared resources, evidence, and authority.

The complete decision cycle begins with a conditional preference, investigates the uncertainty capable of changing it, and revises the strategy across design, funding, construction, and occupancy. Execution then supplies evidence about the chosen path while the alternatives remain available for honest, explicitly simulated comparison.
