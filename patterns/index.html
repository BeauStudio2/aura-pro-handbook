Seven examples

Seven applications of Mission Logic.

Each example applies Mission Logic to a different kind of question. Read the short accounts here, then continue to the capabilities. Open a full example for its decision diagram and reasoning, or read the complete example series in order. One undertaking can use several patterns, and a local inquiry can sit inside a larger decision.

Seven patterns

Clogged sink

When can you stop? · Restoration and verification

A sink drains after the stopper is cleared. Repeating normal use establishes whether the repair worked and whether this finite job can close.

↗

’67 Chevy

What is worth preserving? · Goal discovery and revision

An old wiring modification changes what restoring a car means to its owner. Evidence reveals the choice; the owner decides what is worth preserving.

↗

Attached garage

What else changes? · Design and consequence analysis

Moving a garage opening changes access, loads and runoff. The model exposes connected consequences while another design is still possible.

↗

Duplex portfolio

Does the lesson transfer? · Analogical reasoning and transfer

Several heater failures suggest a pattern. Checking a fourth installation reveals a difference and prevents an unwarranted intervention.

↗

Iron Man suit

Which trial would teach you the most? · Invention and experimental search

A fictional suit’s joint binds under load. A failed prototype identifies a discriminating experiment even though the intended device does not yet work.

↗

Car wash

What needs attention now? · Continuous operations and exception handling

A failed pump closes one car-wash bay. Other bays open, repair and testing follow, and the next morning’s standing checks still come due.

↗

Billion-dollar skyscraper

Which detail changes the whole plan? · Hierarchical planning under uncertainty

A whole-building forecast exposes a local clearance question. Its answer changes the construction sequence, and the revised sequence changes the opening forecast.

Technical detail: compare the patterns and their overlaps

Compare obligations, questions, and methods

The obligation is the outcome owed, the question is the answer needed now, and the method is how to obtain it. One undertaking can use several patterns. How Mission Logic separates these three layers →

PatternTypical obligationQuestion being pursuedMethods that can answer it
Restoration and verificationRestore a known function.Has the function recovered enough to close this issue?Diagnose the gap, intervene, check behavior against the accepted criterion.
Goal discovery and revisionHelp the person shape the desired outcome.Given this discovery, what is worth achieving or preserving?Expose a value conflict, illustrate consequences, elicit the owner's judgment.
Design and consequence analysisChoose a change that serves the purpose.What else would this change affect?Generate alternatives, trace direct and indirect effects, compare tradeoffs.
Analogical reasoning and transferUse prior experience in a present decision.Do the relevant conditions match closely enough for the lesson to apply?Retrieve comparable cases, identify a relevant difference, check it locally.
Invention and experimental searchDevelop a workable method.Which trial would most change what we try next?Propose mechanisms, derive differing predictions, run and interpret a discriminating trial.
Continuous operations and exception handlingSustain a condition over time.What is ready now, and what exception still needs attention?Observe on a schedule or event, isolate a deviation, verify recovery and resume observation.
Hierarchical planning under uncertaintyDeliver a whole outcome across interdependent work.Which local uncertainty could change the best whole-system path?Model dependencies, simulate competing paths, open a focused inquiry, recompute after evidence returns.

Where the patterns overlap

Related processes Shared reasoning The distinction that determines the next move
Restoration and continuous operations Compare observed function with an acceptable condition. A successful check can end a finite problem. In an operation it returns the affected part to service and observation continues.
Goal discovery and invention Let a discovery unsettle the existing plan. If the desired outcome is in question, ask the person what matters. If the means are in question, design a trial.
Design analysis and hierarchical planning Trace dependencies and compare consequences before commitment. Design analysis asks where one change reaches. Hierarchical planning works in both directions: the whole selects a detail, then the detail changes the whole forecast.
Transfer and experimental search Form a hypothesis and seek evidence that distinguishes explanations. Transfer asks whether evidence from elsewhere applies here. Experimentation creates a condition under which competing explanations predict different results.
Transfer and design analysis Use existing knowledge to anticipate effects before acting. Transfer tests comparability between cases. Design analysis tests the relationships among elements within the case.
Hierarchical planning and all six other processes Delegate a smaller reasoning problem to the appropriate process. A component may need restoration, a value decision, a design comparison, an analogy, an experiment, or sustained operation. Its result updates the model at the level where it has consequences.
