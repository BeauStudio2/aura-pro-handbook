Simulation execution and experiments

A simulation follows how the world changes, what people learn about it, and how their responses change what happens next. An experiment compares those responses under stated conditions and checks whether the result deserves to influence a decision.

The air handler supplies a reference calculation. The car wash makes a complete dynamic run visible. The same rules connect thousands of packages in a development.

Case: a separate car-wash experiment

The synthetic morning experiment below tests events, queues and human responses. It is separate from the Tuesday repair in the seven examples. Its final readiness comparison returns to the week-numbered development.

Frame the car-wash experiment

The car-wash example explains how a repair exception sits inside a standing obligation to keep a business operating. This separate illustrative morning asks a narrower question: does promptly reviewing a reported bay fault improve service compared with waiting for the next scheduled review? It does not rewrite the earlier Tuesday-to-Wednesday repair.

The owner and shift lead set the comparison. For the ten customers in this trial, aim to complete at least nine washes by minute 24 and keep mean waiting before the first wash starts at or below two minutes. No response may bypass the repair acceptance, double-book a person, or consume unavailable supplies. Also compare total customer time on site and the attendant’s recovery and receiving time. These are illustrative decision criteria, not recommended operating targets for every car wash.

Two response policies use the same arrivals, equipment condition, and available resources:

  • Routine review: a local equipment stop protects the affected bay; the lead reviews operational exceptions at minute 12 and requests diagnosis then.
  • Prompt review: AP brings the fault report to the lead at minute 7. The lead immediately requests diagnosis within the agreed authority. The minute-12 review remains as a fallback.

Neither policy knows the fault’s cause before diagnosis. The difference is operational review timing, not whether the equipment stops. Diagnosis, repair, testing, and authority requirements are identical.

The runs below were computed with the small reference model linked at the end of this page. Every operating input is synthetic. They demonstrate model execution and comparison; they are not a field trial, a calibrated forecast, or validation of the AP product. Three named scenarios are compared without assigning them probabilities.

Define the world and its rules

Part of the modelReference rule
Starting state and demandThree ready automatic bays, an empty first-in/first-out queue, and ten customers arriving at minutes 0, 2, 4, …, 18. Each completed wash takes six uninterrupted minutes. Bay turnaround is included. No customer abandons the queue in this reference.
AssignmentThe oldest waiting customer takes the lowest-numbered ready bay. Normal wash cycles need no attendant intervention. A customer held in an interrupted bay retains priority there and does not rejoin the arrival queue.
Failure and interruptionIn the fault scenario, bay 2 stops at minute 5 while washing customer 2. The customer remains in the bay under the modeled operating procedure. The old completion event is canceled; a full six-minute wash is required after release. Completed work in other bays remains valid.
Observation and inquiryThe fault report reaches AP at minute 7. Until then, AP has no new report; its last known state is dated, not a fresh confirmation. The report establishes a stopped bay, not a repair diagnosis. The lead acts under the selected review policy.
Repair and acceptanceOne qualified attendant performs one minute of diagnosis, three minutes of the modeled minor repair, and a one-minute functional test. The diagnosis supports that method and the test passes in these runs. The authorized lead accepts the recorded result and releases the bay without an additional modeled review delay. A real procedure supplies its own isolation, qualifications, acceptance, and response times.
Supplies and receivingTwelve usable dose units start on hand. Each wash start, including the interrupted start and its restart, consumes one unit; the functional test consumes one. This is an explicit simplified consumption rule. Six more units arrive at minute 20 and become usable after a two-minute receiving check. Physical arrival alone adds no usable stock.
Resource availabilityThe same attendant diagnoses, repairs, tests, and checks the delivery, with no overlapping assignments. The third scenario keeps that attendant occupied with prior work until minute 14. The receiving job can still begin at minute 20.
Experiment boundaryThe arrival cohort stops at minute 18; all customers are followed through departure, and the delivery is reconciled. This is a terminating experiment, not a claim about a full shift or steady-state capacity. The business’s standing obligation remains open.

Every event changes explicit state. A bay moves through ready, washing, failed, diagnosis, repair, testing, and released states. A person becomes occupied and available. A supply movement changes custody and then accepted inventory. A notification changes someone’s knowledge; it does not repair the bay. The run records these differences rather than replacing them with one generic delay.

The clock advances to the next event. At the same timestamp, the reference processes failures and completed work first, then arrivals, then observations and decisions; it allocates ready bays only after those events are processed. A failure takes precedence over a coincident completion. Resources occupied over an interval are released at its end. This rule makes a minute-12 release usable at minute 12 and prevents insertion order from silently deciding the outcome. Simulation libraries still require explicit time and event-order choices; see SimPy’s scheduling explanation.

That five-minute recovery difference changes later assignments and waiting. AP derives those effects by replaying events, rather than subtracting five minutes from every customer’s finish time.

Initial queue waiting ends at the first wash start. It excludes the interruption experienced by customer 2, so total time on site is reported separately. In the available-attendant fault case, prompt review reduces total initial queue wait from 23 to 18 minutes and total customer time from 98 to 88 minutes. Customer 2 finishes five minutes earlier. Both policies use five attendant minutes for diagnosis, repair, and testing plus two for receiving; the comparison does not invent additional labor.

Supply conservation is also explicit: in either fault run, ten customer starts, one restart, and one functional test consume 12 units. Starting stock 12 plus accepted delivery 6 minus consumption 12 leaves 6. In the no-failure run, ten starts consume 10 and leave 8. A delayed receipt cannot create stock before acceptance; a shortage would block a new start under the model’s allocation rule.

The owner’s illustrative service criteria are met by prompt review in the available-attendant case, but neither policy meets them when the attendant is occupied until minute 14. Earlier information alone cannot release that resource. A different response—another qualified person, changed coverage, or another repair arrangement—would require a new, explicitly resourced alternative.

Decision supported now: prompt review is preferable for the modeled fault when qualified response capacity is available and the stated notification and decision times hold. These runs do not establish an annual benefit, failure probability, false-alert burden, customer abandonment behavior, or an economic case for additional staffing. The three scenarios are not equally likely simply because each appears once.

When repair competes with receiving

The six reference runs above remain unchanged. The following continuation makes the shared attendant and supplies consequential. First, keep the original twelve supply units but make the attendant unavailable until minute 18. Diagnosis, repair, and testing then occupy minutes 18–23. The minute-20 delivery joins the attendant’s work queue, receiving occupies minutes 23–25, and supplies become usable at minute 25. The model now resolves the conflict through waiting rather than stopping at an impossible double booking.

Next, start with eight usable supply units, keeping the same ten arrivals, failure, prompt review, and minute-18 attendant availability. A carrier report gives an expected arrival at minute 20; the shipment actually arrives then in these runs. The lead knows that estimate, not the future outcome. Both policies need the same five-minute non-preemptible recovery job and two-minute receiving check.

Before starting a recovery job, AP reserves one usable dose for its required functional test. The dose becomes consumed when the test runs. Customer starts cannot take reserved stock. This prevents the crew from starting a method whose known acceptance prerequisite is unavailable. If stock is insufficient, the job waits and another ready job can use the attendant. Inventory reconciles usable, reserved, and consumed quantities throughout.

  • Repair first. At minute 18, the lead releases recovery and reserves the last usable dose. Repair and testing finish at minute 23. The shipment has waited since minute 20; the attendant receives it during minutes 23–25. Bay 2 is technically released at minute 23 but its customer’s restart still lacks a dose. Customer 8 and later customers also wait for usable stock. At minute 25, the accepted delivery permits the restart and ready bays to admit queued work.
  • Receive first. The lead holds the attendant available for receiving, based on the reported delivery and current stock. Customer 8 uses the last unreserved dose and starts at minute 18. Receiving runs during minutes 20–22; the accepted stock permits further customer starts and a reserved test dose. Recovery runs during minutes 22–27. If receiving has not begun by minute 22, the hold expires and the model re-evaluates currently ready jobs; it never adds forecast stock to usable inventory.

Receiving first reduces total customer time by 15 minutes and finishes the cohort four minutes earlier, while the held customer leaves two minutes later. The response uses the same person and total task time; the difference comes from sequence, stock, and the resulting queues. Both branches consume twelve units and finish with 8 + 6 − 12 = 2 units.

Neither response meets the original service criteria. AP reports that failure instead of declaring the better aggregate result a success. The lead and owner weigh the held customer’s delay against wider service effects and can consider another qualified receiver or a different supply arrangement. Such an alternative needs actual capacity, authority, and cost; it cannot appear as free help in the simulation.

A failed check changes the response

Keep the receive-first sequence and eight-unit starting stock, but change the first functional test outcome to failure. This is a separate stress case, not evidence that either ordering policy causes a failure. At minute 27, the check does not support release. AP preserves the repair effort and test result, keeps bay 2 unavailable, and opens a new inquiry for the service specialist.

MinuteFinding, owner, and next action
27The attendant reports the failed check. The lead accepts that the bay remains unavailable and authorizes the specialist call within the assumed service arrangement. The original minor-repair method is not repeated automatically.
31–33The booked specialist arrives four minutes after the call with an identified replacement part. A new two-minute diagnosis establishes the component replacement method in this scenario. AP records the new basis and the authorized change of method.
33–37The specialist replaces the component. The part is supplied from the specialist’s available kit; this is a separate assumed resource from the wash-dose inventory. The attendant is free for other work.
37–38A separately reserved dose supports the one-minute retest. The assumed passing result establishes readiness within that check’s scope. The lead accepts it and releases bay 2 at minute 38.
38–44Customer 2 restarts a full wash and departs at minute 44. The other nine customers have already departed by minute 30; independent operation was not held for the repair.

Total customer time increases from 109 to 120 minutes relative to the receive-first passing-test run. Initial queue waiting remains 24 customer-minutes, because the additional delay affects a customer already in service. The attendant still performs seven minutes of recovery and receiving work, but the specialist contributes seven additional on-site minutes, plus the modeled four-minute response interval. AP carries that distinct resource and any resulting expense into the decision; it does not hide it inside the original repair allowance.

The failed first test still consumes its dose. With the specialist retest, total consumption is thirteen units, leaving 8 + 6 − 13 = 1. No failed check releases the bay, and no superseded wash-completion event finishes customer 2 early.

Specialist availability, the replacement part, the new diagnosis, the passing retest, and immediate authorized review are explicit assumptions of this stress case. The model demonstrates a change in method and resource demand after contrary evidence. It does not establish a real failure rate or guarantee that one replacement will resolve every fault. If the part or acceptance remains unavailable, the operating plan must retain that unresolved condition and forecast the consequences.

Build possible futures from evidence

The reference fixes its inputs so the event logic can be inspected. A decision experiment for an operating car wash uses measured arrivals, cycle times, failures, repair histories, stock consumption, staff assignments, and reporting delays. The team supplies missing context and challenges whether the records describe comparable conditions.

UncertaintyHow AP represents it
Supported operating variationFit and check arrival and service behavior against comparable observations. Preserve rush periods, arrival groups, and relevant dependence rather than independently randomizing every event.
Uncertain underlying parametersCarry a plausible productivity or reliability parameter through the relevant run; sample individual events conditional on it. An uncertain slow operating regime should not disappear by drawing an unrelated rate for every task.
Disputed current stateRun alternative starting states, such as a stale stock record or an unresolved fault, until evidence distinguishes them. Do not assign equal probabilities without a basis.
Weakly evidenced disruptionsUse named stress scenarios such as a common utility outage or unavailable technician. Report their consequences separately from calibrated frequencies.
Uncertain model structureCompare explanations or methods, such as restart versus resumable service, and identify the observation that would distinguish them. More repetitions cannot repair a wrong mechanism.

Common causes are represented once and applied where relevant: a water-supply interruption can affect every bay; rain can alter demand and traffic together. An equipment fault caused by operating hours is linked to accumulated use rather than pinned to the same clock time under every policy. The minute-5 fault is an explicit external event only in the reference fixture.

Each policy sees the same underlying demand and external scenarios. Random inputs are keyed to their cause and identity—such as customer, weather episode, or equipment life—so adding a repair event does not shift every later random draw. A shared seed alone does not ensure a fair paired comparison. Policy-dependent consequences, including wear or consumption, are then calculated separately.

When new observations arrive, AP conditions the remaining forecast on them. A repair that has already lasted ten minutes does not restart its original duration distribution. Preserve elapsed work, current condition, completed effort, resource commitments, and what the participant knew. Include interrupted and unresolved cases when estimating durations; missing completion records are not zero-duration successes.

Keep people inside the simulation

A response policy describes what a person can do with the information available at that time. It includes review latency, authority, resource availability, agreed triggers, and alternatives. The simulator may know that a bay will fail at minute 5; the simulated lead does not.

The team can participate in a run, inspect the current dashboard, challenge a diagnosis, or choose another response. AP records the simulated time, information shown, person, choice, and changed assumptions. An exploratory human choice becomes part of a replayable experiment only when its effect is recorded. Reusing the same choice in a different scenario requires a stated rule or another human decision; it cannot silently inherit knowledge of the earlier outcome.

Recommendations, simulated approvals, and hypothetical reservations stay inside the run. Actual spending, staff reassignment, and equipment release still use the live project’s authority. A better simulated outcome does not increase AP’s permission. The owner can value fewer interruptions or predictable staffing over the smallest modeled queue; AP exposes that tradeoff.

Connect models without changing their meaning

The project event engine owns the shared clock, work states, commitments, and resource ledger. Each specialist model owns a declared behavior for its assigned scope. Calls carry the starting state, time interval, units, configuration, boundary conditions, validity limits, and required return. A returned duration replaces the matching allowance; it does not create a second copy of the resource or cost.

Some calculations remain fixed until an input changes. The air-handler clearance or pressure check can be reused only while its configuration and applicability remain valid. Others evolve between events: water storage, temperature, drying, curing, or equipment wear. If such a model predicts a threshold crossing that can change work, the engine schedules that event before allowing dependent work to proceed.

Coupled calculations exchange state at defined times. For example, enclosure changes moisture conditions; drying changes when finishes can start; the selected sequence changes the next enclosure state. Where calculations depend on each other at the same time, use an agreed solution method and convergence criteria. An inconsistent boundary condition, timeout, or failure to converge returns unresolved status rather than a fabricated completion date. Co-simulation concepts such as time advancement, state exchange, and dependencies are described in the FMI specification; using those concepts does not bind AP to that interface.

Refining a work package preserves existing history and the boundary with its parent. AP identifies which aggregate allowance is being replaced, transfers the current state, and checks that resources, quantities, and costs reconcile. If coarse and detailed models disagree materially, the discrepancy becomes an inquiry; greater detail is not automatically greater accuracy.

Test the model and the recommendation

The experiment record names the decision, criteria, starting snapshot, model and policy versions, scenarios, parameter sources, random-input scheme where used, outputs, and review limits. It keeps failures and unsuccessful runs. The reference records each customer, event, attendant interval, inventory total, and outcome so the summary can be reconstructed.

  • Verify the mechanics. Check no duplicate customer, no overlapping resource assignment, no negative stock, and no completion from a canceled wash. Test the no-failure case, a resource that is unavailable, exact deadline ties, and missing acceptance. The reference’s assertions check its stated conservation and paired-result expectations; they do not validate real operating behavior.
  • Validate the behavior. Compare with independent observations of queues, cycles, interruption losses, report delays, repair response, and stock use. A correct final customer count can conceal incorrect waits or compensating errors. If predicted recovery is repeatedly too quick because qualification or review takes longer, revise that mechanism and test on separate cases.
  • Evaluate alternatives without tuning to the answer. Fit behavior on one set of histories, develop policies on separate scenario runs, and compare shortlisted policies on fresh runs. Keep records from the same project or episode together where their dependence would leak information across evaluation sets.
  • Measure decision stability. Report paired differences, uncertainty in those differences, target attainment where probabilities are supported, tail outcomes, effort, and cost. Run until the estimate is precise enough for the actual decision or the decision deadline arrives. Separate random-run error from uncertainty in inputs and model structure.
  • Retain an unresolved answer. If rankings reverse under plausible models, show the conditions and useful next observation. “Best among the tested policies” is not a proof of global optimality. An optimizer’s mathematical bound applies only to its stated formulation and inputs.

No general performance claim follows from the six synthetic runs. A real trial must also capture false alerts, unnecessary checks, intervention cost, abandonment, and conditions omitted from this small model. More runs of the same assumptions reduce sampling error only when sampling exists; they do not make those assumptions true. Credibility remains tied to intended use, as in NASA’s model-and-simulation guidance.

Learning uses the saved prediction and subsequent evidence to identify what changed: starting state, behavior, reporting, human response, or the environment. The lead can learn that fast review helps only with response capacity; AP can learn which availability check deserves attention before recommending it. A lesson remains provisional until comparable outcomes support it, and changes to procedures or collection remain subject to the team’s authority.

Calculate whole-project readiness after local recovery

The same distinction appears in the car wash: a bay can be released while a restart still lacks supplies. For the building, a local installation result must coincide with other accepted prerequisites before testing can start. Using the air-handler reference calendar, testing starts on day 26 and all prerequisites must be complete by the end of day 25.

This is an explicit parent-readiness calculation, not a newly executed whole-building simulation. The package returns its forecast completion day, acceptance still required, resource intervals, changed cost, and input revisions. The parent combines those returns with other prerequisites and the testing calendar. Simulated acceptance states remain simulated; an actual check later updates the live state separately.

In the first case, the planner and commissioning lead can preserve the booking if the recovery and other conditions hold. In the second, AP directs the unresolved readiness question to the controls lead and compares controls recovery or another testing arrangement. The superintendent and relevant commercial owners authorize any changed assignments or commitments. Faster installation alone does not settle the controls question or establish a four-week saving.

A package passes actual resource occupancy, quantities, acceptance states, costs, and remaining uncertainty to its parent. The development model determines whether that change affects finishes, another trade, funding, tests, or occupancy; the portfolio model checks other projects sharing the resource. Parent completion still requires its own integrated criteria. Local improvement is not automatically global improvement.

NVIDIA tools contribute where a bounded calculation changes this comparison: route geometry, supported routing, physical response, or observation processing. The car-wash reference needs none. The CPU event model, specialist services, and optimizer remain replaceable. Their outputs retain the same evidence, authority, and validation requirements.

Reproduce the reference experiment

Download the reference model and inspect the computed inputs, event traces, and results. The small model uses Python 3’s standard library and contains the declared inputs. Running it prints the six scenario-policy runs; it does not connect to any live service or issue commands.

python car-wash-reference.py > car-wash-results.json

The updated model preserves those original six runs and also prints three continuation runs, the minute-18 attendant-conflict check, and the two parent-readiness calculations. The result file separates runs, decision_runs, resource_conflict_check, and parent_readiness. Event traces now distinguish usable stock, test reservations, and consumption. The additional results do not change the original six comparisons.

The model is a finite documentation fixture with the stated failure, repair, and customer rules. It is not a general car-wash simulator or the AP runtime. Its versioned trace makes this chapter’s numerical claims checkable while the broader architecture defines what a project-specific model must represent and validate.
